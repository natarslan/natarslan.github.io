
🎙️ **Narration Script: Strike Weather — The Summer of 1984**


Recently I visited the National Museum in Cardiff. Wandering through the exhibits, I came across a small but striking detail. A sign in the section about the 1984 miners' strike mentioned how miners remembered that summer for its heat. They even had a name for it. Strike weather.

That phrase stuck with me. No temperatures, no graphs. Just a vivid memory, shared by many. And it made me wonder. Was the summer of 1984 really that hot? 

So I started digging.

The backdrop was the miners' strike, which began in March 1984. Led by the National Union of Mineworkers, it wasn’t just about pay. It was about defending entire communities. Towns built around coal mining, held together by generations of hard, dangerous work. In places like South Wales, South Yorkshire, and Lincolnshire, the strike struck deep. It wasn’t just economic. It was personal. Emotional. A fight for survival.

And, in the middle of all that turmoil, a hot, dry summer would only have made everything feel more intense. More vivid. More lasting.

To explore that memory, I turned to data from the UK Met Office. I focused on three key places: Cardiff, Sheffield, and Waddington. Each played a part in the strike. I looked at the summer months June, July, and August. And I compared temperature, rainfall, and sunshine data.

>But to get a better sense of how unusual that summer really was, I used something called a z-score. Think of it like a weirdness meter for weather. It tells us how far a certain season strays from the norm. Not just whether it was hot or dry, but how _strangely_ hot or dry it felt for that time and place.
I used a 30-year baseline from 1977 to 2006, which is pretty standard in climate science. This period gives us a good reference point, before global warming started to speed up dramatically in the late 20th century.
And here’s what I found. Cardiff had a temperature z-score of 0.9. Nearly a full step above average. Significantly sunnier, noticeably drier. Sheffield was less dramatic, but still sunnier than normal. Waddington showed a slight bump too, though nothing extreme.

The data showed something subtle, but real. The summer of 1984 wasn’t a record-breaker. But it was clearly tilted toward the hot and dry end of the spectrum. Especially in Cardiff, where both temperature and sunshine stood out clearly. Rainfall was lower too, which could have made the heat feel more dry, more relentless. Enough to feel different. Enough to feel intense. Especially in places where tensions were already high, and people were living through something extraordinary.

Of course, weather data only goes so far. Humidity matters. So does wind. Those things shape how heat is felt in the body, in the air, in the memory. But even with those limits, it’s fair to say the summer leaned warmer, drier, brighter. Not a heatwave. Just a slow, steady burn.

And there’s another layer here. Climate change.Back then, a summer like 1984 was unusual. Now, it might feel ordinary. What once felt unusual may soon become the new normal.

So here’s where I landed. I didn’t set out to fact-check anyone’s memories, or flatten lived experience into numbers. I just wanted to see how the memories of strike weather lined up with the climate data.

So, that summer really was warmer and drier in many of the places where people were striking. Not extreme, but enough. Enough to be remembered. Enough to become part of the story.Because weather doesn’t just shape the world around us. It shapes how we feel, how we endure, and how we remember.
